# Medication, preventatives, and vet routines

## Timing doses reliably

Twice-daily medication means every 12 hours, not "morning and evening whenever".
Anchor each dose to a fixed event the owner never skips — the human's own
breakfast and bedtime work better than a clock alone. Set a phone alarm for the
dose, not for the reminder to think about the dose.

Medications that must be given with food (most NSAIDs, doxycycline, prednisone)
should be scheduled to overlap a meal, so a med task and a feeding task belong
adjacent in the plan rather than an hour apart. Thyroid medication and some
antibiotics work best on an empty stomach — check the label and, if it conflicts
with mealtime, place the dose 30-60 minutes before food.

## Giving pills without a fight

Pill pockets, a small ball of wet food, or a piece of cheese work for most dogs.
Give two empty treats first, then the loaded one, then a third empty one — most
dogs swallow the loaded treat without inspecting it. For cats, a pill popper
followed immediately by 3-5ml of water by syringe prevents tablets lodging in
the oesophagus. Never dry-pill a cat.

Budget five minutes for a medication task, and ten for a cat that hides. If a
pet consistently refuses, ask the vet about a compounded liquid or transdermal
form rather than fighting daily.

## Missed doses

For a once-daily medication, a dose remembered within a few hours can usually be
given late; if it is nearly time for the next one, skip it and resume the
schedule. Never double up to catch up. Missed doses of anti-seizure or heart
medication are an exception — call the vet the same day.

## Monthly and seasonal preventatives

Flea, tick, and heartworm preventatives are typically monthly. Pick a fixed
calendar day (the 1st, or the day the pet was adopted) and set a recurring
reminder; efficacy drops off at the end of the interval, and a gap of even a few
weeks in heartworm coverage during mosquito season is meaningful. Ticks are
active any time the temperature is above about 4C/40F, so year-round coverage is
now standard in most regions.

## Routine vet visits

Healthy adult pets need an annual wellness exam; seniors (dogs over 7, cats over
10) benefit from every six months, with bloodwork to catch kidney, thyroid, and
liver changes early. Bring a list of current medications, a stool sample if
requested, and a two-week log of any symptom you want investigated — appetite,
water intake, or bathroom changes are far more useful to a vet as a written
record than as a recollection.

## When to seek care the same day

Repeated vomiting, straining to urinate (especially in male cats — this is an
emergency), a distended hard abdomen, collapse, pale gums, seizures, laboured
breathing, or a sudden refusal to eat lasting more than 24 hours all warrant
prompt veterinary attention rather than a wait-and-see approach.
