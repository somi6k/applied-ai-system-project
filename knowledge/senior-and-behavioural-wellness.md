# Senior pets, sleep, and behavioural wellness

## Adjusting routines for senior pets

Dogs are generally senior from about age 7 (earlier for giant breeds, later for
small ones); cats from about 10. Replace one long walk with two or three shorter
ones on soft ground, keep sessions gentle but frequent — inactivity accelerates
muscle loss and stiffness. Warm up with five minutes of slow walking before any
play. Watch for difficulty with stairs, hesitation before jumping, or a change
in sleeping position, all early arthritis signs.

Add non-slip runners on hard floors, raise food and water bowls for large dogs,
and provide a low-sided litter box for arthritic cats. Night-time pacing,
staring at walls, or reversed sleep-wake cycles in an old pet can indicate
cognitive dysfunction, which is treatable — mention it to the vet rather than
writing it off as ageing.

## Sleep and rest requirements

Adult dogs sleep 12-14 hours a day and puppies up to 20; cats 12-16. Chronic
under-sleeping shows up as irritability and poor impulse control, and is common
in busy households with young children. Protect a quiet, uninterrupted rest
block in the middle of the day. A crate-trained or "place"-trained dog with an
enforced nap after morning exercise is noticeably calmer in the evening.

## Structure and predictability

Pets read routine rather than clocks, but they are sensitive to sequence. Keep
the order of daily events stable — walk, then breakfast, then rest — even when
the times shift. Sudden schedule changes (a new work pattern, a house move) are
best introduced over one to two weeks, shifting by 15 minutes a day.

## Training as wellness

Five to ten minutes of reward-based training a day covers a surprising amount of
mental exercise, and short daily sessions beat one weekly class for retention.
Useful default behaviours: a settle on a mat, a reliable recall, cooperative
handling for nail trims and ear checks. Training a pet to accept grooming and
vet handling reduces stress at every future appointment.

## Separation and alone time

Build alone time deliberately: leave for 30 seconds, then two minutes, then ten,
always returning before the pet becomes distressed. A food puzzle given at
departure creates a positive association. Signs of genuine separation anxiety —
destruction near exits, drooling, non-stop vocalising, house soiling in an
otherwise trained pet — need a behaviour plan rather than more exercise alone.

## Multi-pet households

Feed pets separately if there is any resource guarding. Introduce new animals
through scent swapping and barrier meetings over one to two weeks before free
access. Each pet needs some one-on-one owner time daily; ten focused minutes per
pet prevents the quieter animal's needs from being absorbed by the demanding one
when the owner's time is tight.
